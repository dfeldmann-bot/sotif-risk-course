From acceptance criterion to validation target

One formula turns “at most this much harm per hour” into “drive this many hours”. You need to be able to derive it, and to say what it quietly assumes.

Why this, first. Every quantitative claim in your residual-risk argument hangs from this link in the chain. Scenario weighting (lesson 4), copulas (5) and SINADRA runtime models (7) all exist to make this one number reachable. If the base derivation is wrong, nothing built on it holds.

Predict first

Schaller et al. use the example criterion “overlooking a car on the other lane at most 10⁻⁹ per hour”.1 How many failure-free operating hours would show that at 95% confidence? Write your order-of-magnitude guess down before you read on. You will check it in §2.

1 Schaller et al., Discover AI 6:1172 (2026), §5.1. They note this is the order of magnitude civil aviation uses for “extremely improbable” and call the testing needed “prohibitive”.

1 · The chain

ISO 21448 sets an acceptance criterion AH for each harm H (Clause 6). Clause 9 then turns it into one or more validation targets: test effort with pass/fail criteria. Annex C.2 gets there by peeling the harm rate back to the rate of the hazardous behaviour (HB) that the system itself controls:2

2 Formula and variable reading as reported by Putze et al. (DLR, 2023), §III, and Betschinske et al. (2025), Eq. 6. Check the wording against your licensed copy of Annex C.2.
AH = RHB · P(E | HB) · P(C̄ | E) · P(S | C̄) (1)

Solve for RHB. Every factor below 1 that you can justify buys test effort. If only 10% of hazardous behaviours occur in an exposing scenario, the system may misbehave ten times more often than the harm criterion allows. The same applies to the other two factors.

Assumption 0: the factorsThe factors in (1) are taken from field data and treated as properties of the world. Putze et al. point out that they are not: exposure depends on your route choice and ODD, and controllability depends on your fallback behaviour. Each factor needs its own justification for your system. The notation also hides conditioning. Severity really depends on the event and the behaviour too, so the correct term is P(S | C̄, E, HB).

2 · The zero-failure test

Model hazardous behaviours as a Poisson process with rate R. After τ hours the expected count is λ = Rτ. You drive τ hours and see none. The test is a hypothesis test of H₀: rate ≥ RHB. Reject H₀ if seeing zero events would be improbable under it:

P(0 events | RHB) = e−RHBτ ≤ 1 − C  ⇒  τ = −ln(1 − C) / RHB (2)

At C = 0.95, −ln 0.05 ≈ 3.00. This is the reliability engineer’s rule of three: you need about three times the inverse target rate, with zero events. RAND’s famous result is exactly this formula. 1.09 fatalities per 10⁸ miles gives about 275 million failure-free miles.3

3 Kalra & Paddock, RAND RR-1478 (2016). They use n = ln(1−C)/ln(1−F), which equals (2) to first order in F.
Notation trapIn Putze et al.’s rendering of Annex C, “α” in (2) is the confidence level. In Betschinske et al., α is the type-I error, so their formula reads −ln(α). This course always writes C for confidence. When an assessor quotes “α = 0.05”, ask which one they mean.
Check your §0 prediction

τ = 3.00 / 10⁻⁹ h⁻¹ ≈ 3.0 × 10⁹ h. That is about 342,000 years for one vehicle running 24/7, or 34 years for a fleet of 10,000. This is why Schaller et al. turn to monitor outputs as proxy evidence. It is also why ISO 21448 lets you argue absence of unreasonable risk by means other than direct failure probabilities.

3 · What each observed failure costs

A plan may allow k events. The pass condition becomes P(X ≤ k | λ) ≤ 1 − C. The resulting λk(C) equals half the χ² quantile with 2(k+1) degrees of freedom:

The catch is that k and τ must be fixed before you drive. If you see one event and then decide to “just extend to the k = 1 length”, you have changed the test after looking at the data. That inflates the chance of passing a system that misses its target.4 Lesson 2 covers the designs that do allow stopping early: Wald’s sequential test and Bayesian updating.

Try it: set k to 1, then C to 0.99, and watch the fleet years. Then set P(E | HB) to 1. That is what you are left with if the exposure factor cannot be defended.

4 · What a passed test silently assumes

  1. Homogeneous Poisson. A constant rate and independent events. In reality the rate varies with road type, weather and traffic density, so the result is an average over whatever mix you happened to drive.
  2. Representative exposure. The test mix of conditions matches the deployed mix. Your test fleet is not your customer fleet.
  3. Frozen software. Every hour was driven on the release candidate. A fix resets the count unless you argue why old hours still apply.
  4. One behaviour per harm. If several HBs lead to H, their rates add. Annex C.2 leaves this aggregation out without flagging it (Putze et al.). Lesson 3 covers it.
  5. Frequentist meaning. “95%” means a system exactly at the limit would pass at most 5% of the time. It does not mean P(rate < R) = 0.95 unless you state a prior.
