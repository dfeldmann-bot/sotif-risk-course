Decomposition

(1)Annex C.2 factorisation of the acceptance criterion

\[A_H = R_{HB}\cdot P(E\mid HB)\cdot P(\bar C\mid E)\cdot P(S\mid \bar C)\]

The harm rate is the hazardous-behaviour rate multiplied by the chance it meets an exposing scenario, is not controlled, and reaches severity S.

\[A_H = 1.00\times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{h^{-1}},\quad P(E\mid HB)=0.1,\ P(\bar C\mid E)=0.5,\ P(S\mid\bar C)=0.2\]

Source: ISO 21448:2022 Annex C.2, as rendered by Putze et al. 2023 (arXiv:2304.10170)

(2)Tolerable hazardous-behaviour rate

\[R_{HB} = \frac{A_H}{P(E\mid HB)\,P(\bar C\mid E)\,P(S\mid \bar C)}\]

Solve the factorisation for the rate the system itself controls. Each justified factor below 1 relaxes the target.

\[R_{HB} = \frac{1.00\times 10^{-7}}{0.1\cdot0.5\cdot0.2} = 1.00\times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{h^{-1}}\]

Source: Betschinske et al. 2025 (arXiv:2506.10363), Eq. 6

(3)Correct conditioning (chain rule)

\[P(H) = P(HB)\,P(E\mid HB)\,P(\bar C\mid E,HB)\,P(S\mid \bar C,E,HB)\]

Annex C.2 drops conditioning. Using P(S | C̄) silently assumes severity is independent of the scenario and the behaviour, which you would have to justify.

Source: Putze et al. 2023, §III

Zero-failure test

(4)Poisson model for hazardous behaviours

\[P(X = n \mid \lambda) = \frac{\lambda^{n} e^{-\lambda}}{n!},\qquad \lambda = R\,\tau\]

Expected count after τ hours of exposure at constant rate R (homogeneous Poisson process).

Source: Standard; Annex C.3 per Betschinske et al. 2025

(5)Hypotheses

\[H_0:\ R > A_H \qquad H_1:\ R \le A_H\]

Passing the test rejects H₀. In Lesson 1 the test is applied at the behaviour level, with R_HB as the threshold.

Source: Betschinske et al. 2025, Eq. 3

(6)Pass condition with zero observed events

\[P(X=0\mid R_{HB}\tau) = e^{-R_{HB}\tau} \le 1-C\]

A system exactly at the limit would show zero events with probability at most 1 − C.

Source: Lesson 1, Eq. 2

(7)Validation target (k = 0)

\[\tau = \frac{-\ln(1-C)}{R_{HB}}\]

Required failure-free exposure. Notation: Putze et al. write α for the confidence C; Betschinske et al. write α for 1 − C, so their form is −ln(α)/A_H.

\[\tau = \frac{-\ln(1-0.95)}{1.00\times 10^{-5}} = \frac{2.9957}{1.00\times 10^{-5}} = 2.996\times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{h}\]

Source: ISO 21448 Annex C (per Putze et al. 2023); Betschinske et al. 2025, Eq. 5

(8)Rule of three

\[-\ln(1-0.95) = \ln 20 \approx 2.996 \;\Rightarrow\; \tau \approx \frac{3}{R_{HB}}\]

At 95% confidence you need about three times the inverse target rate, with zero events.

Source: Standard reliability result

(9)RAND exact form (per-mile Bernoulli trials)

\[n = \frac{\ln(1-C)}{\ln(1-F)} \;\approx\; \frac{-\ln(1-C)}{F}\quad (F \ll 1)\]

Kalra & Paddock treat each mile as a trial with failure probability F. It agrees with the Poisson form to first order in F.

\[F = 1.09\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{mi^{-1}},\ C=0.95:\quad n = 2.748\times 10^{8}\ \text{failure-free miles}\]

Source: Kalra & Paddock, RAND RR-1478 (2016), Eq. 2

(10)Schaller et al. example

\[R = 10^{-9}\ \mathrm{h^{-1}},\ C = 0.95:\quad \tau = \frac{2.996}{10^{-9}}\]

Their example criterion for overlooking a car on the other lane. This is why they argue for monitor outputs as proxy evidence.

\[\tau = 3.00\times 10^{9}\ \mathrm{h} \approx 341{,}744\ \text{vehicle-years (24/7)}\]

Source: Schaller et al. 2026, Discover AI 6:1172, §5.1

Plans allowing k events

(11)Pass condition with up to k events

\[P(X \le k \mid \lambda) = \sum_{i=0}^{k} \frac{\lambda^{i}e^{-\lambda}}{i!} \le 1 - C\]

Fix k and τ before driving. Changing either after seeing data inflates the type-I error.

Source: Betschinske et al. 2025, Eq. 4

(12)Poisson–χ² identity

\[\lambda_k(C) = \tfrac12\,\chi^2_{2(k+1)}(C), \qquad \tau_k = \frac{\lambda_k(C)}{R_{HB}}\]

The λ that solves the pass condition with equality is half the χ² quantile with 2(k+1) degrees of freedom.

\[k=0,\ C=0.95:\quad \lambda_k = 2.9957,\quad \tau_k = 2.996\times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{h}\]

Source: Standard (Poisson–gamma–χ² relation)

(13)Calendar time for a test fleet

\[T_{\text{cal}} = \frac{\tau_k}{N_{\text{fleet}}\cdot h_{\text{yr}}}\]

Converts exposure into calendar years for a given fleet.

\[T_{\text{cal}} = \frac{2.996\times 10^{5}}{50\cdot3000} = 2.00\ \text{years}\]

Source: Lesson 1 calculator

Scaling

(14)Price of confidence

\[\frac{\tau(C_2)}{\tau(C_1)} = \frac{\ln(1-C_2)}{\ln(1-C_1)}\]

τ grows only logarithmically with confidence but linearly with 1/R.

\[\frac{\tau(0.99)}{\tau(0.95)} = \frac{\ln 0.01}{\ln 0.05} = 1.537\]

Source: Lesson 1, Exercise 3

(15)Price of allowing k events

\[\frac{\tau_k}{\tau_0} = \frac{\lambda_k(C)}{-\ln(1-C)}\]

At 95%, one allowed event costs 58% more exposure.

\[\frac{\tau_1}{\tau_0}\Big|_{C=0.95} = \frac{4.744}{2.996} = 1.584\]

Source: Lesson 1, §3

Aggregation

(16)Bonferroni-type bound over triggering condition → behaviour → event paths

\[P(H) \le \sum_{T,B,E} P(T)\,P(B\mid T)\,P(E\mid B,T)\,P(H\mid E,B,T)\]

Several paths can lead to the same harm, and their contributions add. Annex C.2 omits this aggregation, so the budget A_H must be allocated across paths.

Source: Putze et al. 2023, Eq. 2

(17)Joint confidence over several validation targets

\[C_{\text{joint}} \ge 1 - \sum_i (1 - C_i)\]

If you validate m paths separately, the confidence that all of them hold is at least this (Bonferroni).

\[m = 3,\ C_i = 0.95:\quad C_{\text{joint}} \ge 0.85\]

Source: Betschinske et al. 2025 (decomposition of hazardous behaviour)

Assumptions behind every result above

  1. Homogeneous Poisson process: constant rate, independent events.
  2. Representative exposure: the test mix of conditions equals the deployed mix.
  3. Frozen software, or an argued carry-over of hours across fixes.
  4. All paths to harm H are counted, with the budget allocated (Eq. 16–17).
  5. Frequentist confidence: not P(rate < R) = C unless you state a prior.
