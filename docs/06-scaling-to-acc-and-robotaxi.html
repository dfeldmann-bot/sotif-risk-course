Scaling up: ACC and L4 robotaxi

What changes when the same method is applied to ACC and then to an L4 urban robotaxi, and a staged roadmap.

1. Scenario-based residual risk2. Field data and SPIs3. Gaussian copula4. How the 1,000 scenarios were generated5. Unintended braking report6. Scaling up: ACC and L4 robotaxi7. Scenario database, ODD coverage and completeness

The unintended-braking example has one hazardous behaviour, one harm, one other agent and seven parameters. Real functions have many of each. The estimator does not change. What changes is the scenario space, the interaction between agents, and the cost of exploring rare regions.

2 · ACC: what to add

  1. HB catalogue from the SOTIF analysis, each with its triggering conditions (radar multipath under bridges, stationary-object filtering, curve target selection, cut-in detection latency).
  2. Scenario types per HB, as a partition of the ODD by manoeuvre (follow / cut-in / cut-out / approach) × road × speed band × traffic density.
  3. Two-sided harm: late braking risks hitting the lead; phantom braking risks being hit. Sum per harm, not per HB.
  4. Driver in the loop: \(P(\bar C\mid E)\) becomes a driver-response model (take-over time, brake force). It is also uncertain, and also needs a copula with the scenario parameters.
  5. Parameters with dependence: lead deceleration, time gap, relative speed and cut-in lateral speed are coupled. Fit them from naturalistic data (e.g. highD-type datasets, if the ODD matches).
  6. Sampling: about 15 dimensions. Fixed-proposal IS starts to lose efficiency (Figure 1); use cross-entropy to adapt the proposal.

3 · L4 robotaxi: what changes fundamentally

  • Scenario space is open. The taxonomy will never be complete, so the unknown-scenario term \(R_{\text{unknown}}\) and its SPIs (discovery rate, Good–Turing \(n_1/N\)) become a main part of the argument, not a footnote.
  • Closed-loop, multi-agent. Outcomes depend on how other agents react to the robotaxi. Reactive agent models enter \(P(\text{HB}\mid\theta)\) and must be validated themselves.
  • Perception error models are central. Simulating raw sensors at fidelity is rarely credible, so P(HB) is computed from planner replays with injected, statistically modelled perception errors (by class, range, occlusion, weather).
  • High dimension. Fixed importance sampling collapses (Figure 1). Use adaptive or sequential methods: cross-entropy, subset simulation (Au & Beck 2001), and surrogate-guided search (Gaussian-process or learned failure-boundary models), always with a final unbiased estimate and ESS.
  • Acceptance criterion. Positive risk balance per harm type against a human benchmark matched to the ODD (ISO/TS 5083), with the rules from lesson 1 still applied to each claim.
  • Runtime evidence. Situation-aware risk monitors (Reich's SINADRA) and monitor ensembles (Schaller et al.) supply leading SPIs and runtime restrictions. They reduce \(\nu_\ell\) for risky situations, and that reduction must be argued in the same equation.
\[ w = \prod_{i=1}^d \frac{\varphi(x_i)}{\varphi(x_i-\delta)}, \qquad E_q[w^2] = e^{d\delta^2}, \qquad \frac{\text{ESS}}{N} \approx \frac{1}{E_q[w^2]} = e^{-d\delta^2} \]

4 · A staged roadmap

  1. One HB, one harm (this example): build the data pipeline, the evidence cells and the SPI loop end to end.
  2. ACC: several HBs and both rear-end directions; driver response model; fitted copulas; cross-entropy IS; per-harm aggregation.
  3. L4 pilot ODD: a geofenced, limited ODD with a few hundred scenario types; perception error models; reactive agents; unknown-scenario SPIs; runtime risk monitors.
  4. L4 scale-out: extend the ODD only where scenario coverage, exposure data and SPIs support it, re-running the risk sum per extension.
