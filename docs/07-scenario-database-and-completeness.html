1 · The vehicle-level V&V strategy for L4

Terminology: ISO 21448 reserves “vehicle-level SOTIF strategy” (VLSS) for a set of vehicle-level requirements, verified according to the Clause 9 V&V strategy (3.34, p. 10; D.1, p. 159). What follows is that Clause 9 strategy. ISO 21448 divides scenarios into known not hazardous (area 1), known hazardous (2), unknown hazardous (3) and unknown not hazardous (4) areas (4.2.2, pp. 12–14). Validation shows that the known-unsafe and unknown-unsafe areas carry acceptable residual risk. For L4, complement it with ISO/TS 5083 (ADS level; positive risk balance is already a rationale in ISO 21448 6.5, p. 30), UL 4600 (SPIs, safety case) and, for EU type approval, the UNECE NATM pillars (audit, simulation, track, real world, in-service monitoring; ECE/TRANS/WP.29/2021/61, Regulation (EU) 2022/1426).

2 · What the scenario database contributes

  1. Makes “known” explicit. The taxonomy is the boundary between known and unknown, and logical scenarios must partition the ODD.
  2. Holds the weights: \(\nu_\ell\) and \(f_\ell(\theta)\) from recordings.
  3. Generates tests (logical → concrete, e.g. OpenSCENARIO) with traceability triggering condition → HB → HE → requirement → test.
  4. Shows coverage: which cells (scenario × triggering condition × software version) have evidence.
  5. Stores every run with \(\theta\), \(q(\theta)\), software hash and outcome, so the risk can be recomputed after each change.
  6. Moves unknown to known: newly discovered types become categories, and their arrival rate is a Clause 11 SPI.

  • Extract scenarios with a reference world model. If the stack under test decides which scenarios exist, it cannot miss its own failures.
  • Tag every driven second, even when no scenario happens. Otherwise \(\nu\) has no denominator.
  • Provenance (software hash, map, calibration) and a pointer back to the raw log window on every occurrence.
  • Version the taxonomy and re-tag historical data when it changes.

4 · Identifying scenarios automatically

Known types: tag-based extraction. Tag each actor's activities (braking, lane change, turning) and relations (lead vehicle, cut-in, crossing). A category is a tag combination, and \(\theta\) is read at an anchor event, e.g. lane crossing for a cut-in (Guo, Muñoz Sánchez, de Gelder, van der Sande 2023, on the Waymo Open Motion Dataset). The tagger is a classifier, so validate it against a human-labelled sample and correct the exposure:

\[ \hat\nu_\ell = \frac{n_{\text{detected}}\cdot \text{precision}}{\text{recall}\cdot T} \]

New types: Clause 11 discovery. (1) A queue of unexplained critical events (low TTC, hard braking, HB flags, monitor flags, disengagements, remote assistance) that match no category goes to human review. (2) Unsupervised clustering of time-window embeddings, with outliers far from all known clusters flagged. (3) ODD novelty: conditions outside the spec, or rare tag combinations.

5 · ODD tags: coverage and granularity

ISO 34503 gives the ODD taxonomy (scenery, environmental conditions, dynamic elements). ISO 34504 gives scenario-categorisation tags. Both are a shared vocabulary, not a sufficient resolution: ISO 34504 offers examples rather than prescribed values, and does not fix thresholds such as what “moving faster” means. Two things must be added for your function.

Missing tags

Risk-relevant attributes for your triggering conditions are often not standard tags: overhead structures, road spray and low-sun angle for phantom braking, for example. Derive extra tags from the Clause 7 triggering-condition analysis, from SINADRA-style structured environment models (guide phrases over an environment ontology identify the factors that change a hazardous situation's risk; Reich 2026), and from unexplained field events. Map each custom tag to a parent in ISO 34503/34504 so the vocabulary stays exchangeable.

Granularity: how fine is fine enough?

A class may lump values together as long as a shift of the distribution within the class cannot move the risk materially. With conditional harm \(p(\theta)\), class exposure \(\nu_c\), and test vs. deployment distributions inside the class:

\[ \big|\Delta R_c\big| = \nu_c\left|\int_c p(\theta)\,\big(f^{\text{dep}}_c - f^{\text{test}}_c\big)\,d\theta\right| \;\le\; \nu_c\,\big(p^{\max}_c - p^{\min}_c\big)\,\mathrm{TV}\big(f^{\text{dep}}_c, f^{\text{test}}_c\big) \]

Here \(\mathrm{TV}(f,g)=\tfrac12\int|f-g|\) (total variation); read as the full \(L^1\) distance the bound would be off by a factor 2. Refine a class until \(\sum_c \nu_c\,(p^{\max}_c-p^{\min}_c)\,\mathrm{TV}^{\max}_c \le \delta\), a reserved share of the budget. The rule refines where harm varies steeply and exposure is high. Boundaries must also be measurable in logs and at runtime, otherwise the class cannot be tagged or monitored.

Combinatorial coverage

Coverage of single tags is not enough; triggering conditions are often combinations. Count evidence per tag pair (or t-wise) and saturate at a minimum \(n\) (tag-based coverage, Coverage Metrics for a Scenario Database…, arXiv:2409.01139):

\[ \text{Coverage}_{\text{tag}}(n) = \frac{1}{n\,|L|\,|C|}\sum_{L}\sum_{C} \min\big(n, N(L,C)\big) \]

6 · Arguing completeness

Completeness of a scenario concept cannot be proven; it is argued, typically in GSN, by refuting counter-hypotheses with evidence (Glasmacher, Weber, Eckstein 2024). Completeness is binary and applies to the concept; coverage is quantitative and applies to parameter spaces and value assignments. As they put it, “unforeseen scenarios cannot be identified with argumentation, but with external input”. Use five legs:

  1. By construction: taxonomy derived systematically (ISO 34503 attributes × guidewords, SEM, triggering-condition analysis), reviewed by independent experts; atomicity of base scenarios.
  2. Explained time (Type II coverage): the share of driven time assigned to some category. Glasmacher et al. report no unassigned seconds at base-scenario level on inD. Every unassigned second is a candidate unknown.
  3. Saturation: the discovery curve flattens, and the estimated missing mass is small.
  4. Risk-weighted remainder: what matters is not the number of missing types but their risk. Bound it with the Clause 11 field evidence (lesson 1) inside the reserve \(\varepsilon A_H\).
  5. Kept open in operation (Cl. 13): discovery rate, unexplained-event rate and tagger recall as SPIs, since completeness claims expire.
\[ \hat M_0 = \frac{f_1}{N}\ \ (\text{Good–Turing}), \qquad \hat S = S_{\text{obs}} + \frac{f_1(f_1-1)}{2(f_2+1)}\ \ (\text{Chao1}), \qquad R_{\text{unknown}} \lesssim \hat M_0\,\nu_{\text{tot}}\,\bar p_{\text{unk}} \]

\(f_1, f_2\): number of types seen exactly once or twice; \(N\): occurrences; \(\nu_{\text{tot}}\): total scenario occurrence rate; \(\bar p_{\text{unk}}\): harm per unknown occurrence, bounded conservatively or by field driving. Hauer et al. (2019) frame the same question as “did we test all scenario types?”.

\(\hat M_0\) is a point estimate, not a bound. The missing mass satisfies \(P(U_N>E\,U_N+\varepsilon)\le e^{-N\varepsilon^2}\) (Berend & Kontorovich 2013, Theorem 1), so an upper bound at confidence \(1-\delta\) adds about \(\sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/N}\). With \(N=200{,}000\) and \(\delta=0.05\) that term is \(3.9\times10^{-3}\), larger than \(\hat M_0\) itself: saturation data bound the unknown mass only weakly.

They complement each other: ISO 34503/34504 (plus your custom tags) form the ontology; OpenLABEL stores the per-recording, per-interval annotations that reference it; the scenario database indexes them; OpenSCENARIO describes executable tests. The OpenLABEL concept paper lists BSI PAS 1883 and ASAM OpenODD as related standards. The mapping to ISO 34504 is yours to define.
